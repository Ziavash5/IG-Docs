# Should a German company open a Canadian branch or a subsidiary?

**Direct answer.** For most German companies establishing a lasting presence in Canada,
a **Canadian subsidiary** (a corporation incorporated in Canada) is the common
structure, because it ring-fences liability and is taxed as a Canadian resident. A
**branch** keeps the operation inside the German parent's legal entity. The right choice
turns on liability, how profits are taxed on both the Canadian *and* German sides, and
treaty treatment. _(Each assertion below is tied to an official source; interpretive
trade-offs are general information — your situation is decided on a call.)_

## The rule (Canada side)

- A subsidiary is incorporated under the **Canada Business Corporations Act**. [`justice-cbca`]
- A branch is the German parent **carrying on business in Canada** directly; its
  Canadian-source business income is taxed, and **branch tax** applies under the Income
  Tax Act. [`justice-ita` — VERIFY section]
- Treaty relief: branch tax may be reduced/eliminated under the **Canada–Germany Tax
  Treaty**. [`fin-ca-de-treaty` — VERIFY article]

## What determines how it applies

- Expected liability exposure of the Canadian activity.
- Whether the activity creates a **permanent establishment** (interpretive — booked call).
- Repatriation plans (dividends from a subsidiary vs. branch profit remittance).
- Whether early Canadian losses are wanted on the German books.

## How it differs for your corridor (DACH)

> This is the part a Canada-only competitor cannot write.

- **German-side consequence.** A branch's Canadian result flows into the German parent's
  accounts; a subsidiary is a separate taxpayer whose profits face German tax only on
  distribution / under CFC rules. German CFC (Außensteuergesetz) treatment is the
  decisive variable. [`de-bzst` — VERIFY]
- **Treaty interaction.** The Canada–Germany treaty governs branch tax and dividend
  withholding differently. [`fin-ca-de-treaty` — VERIFY]
